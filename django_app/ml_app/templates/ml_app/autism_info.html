What is Autism?

Understanding Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) — A comprehensive guide featuring information from leading medical and research organizations.

Definition

Autism, or Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), refers to a broad range of conditions characterized by challenges with social skills, repetitive behaviors, speech and nonverbal communication.

According to the Centers for Disease Control (CDC), autism affects an estimated 1 in 31 children and 1 in 45 adults in the United States today.

Many Types of Autism

There is not one type of autism, but many. Autism looks different for everyone, and each person with autism has a distinct set of strengths and challenges.

Communication Differences

Some autistic people can speak, while others are nonverbal or minimally verbal and communicate in other ways.

Cognitive & Support Needs

Some have intellectual disabilities while others do not. Some require significant support in daily life, while others need less support or live independently.

Unique Strengths

Each person brings unique strengths and abilities. Many autistic individuals excel in areas like pattern recognition, attention to detail, systematic thinking, and specialized knowledge.

When is Autism Typically Diagnosed?

On average, autism is diagnosed around age 5 in the United States, with signs often appearing by age 2 or 3.

However, diagnosis can occur at any point in a person's life. Many adults receive an autism diagnosis later in life, sometimes after years of struggling with undiagnosed challenges.

Support Levels (DSM-5-TR)

Current diagnostic guidelines break down autism diagnosis into three levels based on the amount of support a person might need:

Level 1: Requiring Support

Individuals require some support with social and communication challenges, may have some repetitive behaviors, and typically can live independently with appropriate accommodations.

Level 2: Requiring Substantial Support

Individuals have more noticeable challenges with social and communication skills, more repetitive behaviors, and typically need more substantial support to function in daily life.

Level 3: Requiring Very Substantial Support

Individuals have significant challenges with verbal and nonverbal communication and require very substantial support to function in all aspects of daily life.

Co-occurring Conditions

Many people with autism experience other medical, behavioral, or mental health conditions that can affect their quality of life. Among the most common are:

ADHD

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder

Anxiety & Depression

Anxiety disorders and depression

GI Disorders

Gastrointestinal complications

Seizures

Seizure disorders and epilepsy

Sleep Disorders

Sleep disturbances and insomnia

Autism Across Demographics

Anybody can be autistic, regardless of sex, age, race, or ethnicity. However, research shows important differences in how autism manifests and is diagnosed:

Boys vs. Girls

Boys get diagnosed with autism approximately 4 times more often than girls. Girls may have more subtle presentation of symptoms, fewer social and communication challenges, and fewer repetitive behaviors. Their symptoms may go unrecognized by doctors, leading to underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis.

Adults with Autism

Getting a diagnosis is often harder for autistic adults, who frequently learn to "mask" or hide their autism symptoms. Many adults discover they are autistic later in life, sometimes after decades of unmet support needs.

Autism is Lifelong

Autism is a lifelong condition, and an autistic person's needs, strengths, and challenges may change over time. As they transition through life stages, they may need different types of support and accommodations.

Early intervention and therapies can make a significant difference in a person's skills and outcomes later in life. However, support needs evolve throughout the lifespan, from early childhood through adulthood.
